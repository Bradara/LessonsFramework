# Why Do Your Big Goals Feel Impossible?

You can be at the bottom of the mountain. You can stare at the summit for as long as you want. You can visualize how you’ll feel when you get to the top. But that can’t get you to the top.

A very dear friend of mine has achieved something I can’t even imagine in my head. 

He has climbed Mount Everest. Twice. 

I haven’t seen him for a decade now, so it’s possible he’s done it for the third time!

I was always fascinated by his stories. One day, I asked him, "How do you even train for something like that?" And he said that part of his training involved walking on his treadmill, with a heavy backpack, at the steepest slope setting... for six hours a day! 

I asked him what he remembered from his actual climb. He said, "When you're climbing Everest, you’re always out of breath. But I learned not to keep looking up. I’d keep my head down. Take one step. Then wait for a minute to catch your breath. Then take the next step. And then wait until you’re ready to take the next one." 

Because if you look up, the mountain breaks you.

That is how big goals feel to the brain. Train for a marathon. Write a book. Start a business. Fix your finances. Change your career path. Each one feels like such an inspiring goal. But up close, they can break you. 

This is why habits are so incredibly important. They stop our brain from pretending that it can climb the entire mountain in one big leap.

Big goals create anxiety because they are abstract. Small, boring habits reduce anxiety because they are concrete. 

A habit is the next step you can still take when the mountain feels too big.

A habit keeps your head down. 

ONE INSIGHT

Everyone tells you to visualize success. “Manifest your reality in your head and the universe will find a way to make it real.”

That’s just silly. The universe doesn’t give a damn about what’s in my head or yours. It doesn’t care about fulfilling a list of wishes that you so vividly visualize every morning in your “power up” session. 

In fact, reality works the opposite way: people who vividly visualize success can become *less* likely to achieve it than those who don't. 

Shocking, but that's what NYU researchers found after twenty years of work.

So dreaming harder and waiting for the Universe to make way for your highway to success is not a good strategy. It’s not even a strategy. 

ONE TOOL

In 1900, a physicist named [Max Planck](https://www.mpg.de/19252749/what-did-max-planck-discover?utm_source=sandeepswadia.beehiiv.com&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=why-do-your-big-goals-feel-impossible) discovered that energy doesn't flow in a smooth, continuous stream. It moves in tiny, discrete packets. He called it a quantum. It was so radical an idea that even Planck himself didn't believe the mathematics behind it. But that idea became one of the foundations of quantum mechanics.

The hardest part of any habit is starting it. In physics, this is called static friction; it takes more force to start than to keep going. The first push requires more force than every push after. If you want to write a book, writing the first line is the hardest. Want to clean the kitchen? Picking up the first plate is the hardest. Want to work out? Putting on your shoes is the hardest part.

**So start quantizing. Shrink the action to the smallest possible unit.** Now this doesn’t mean that you’re lowering the bar. That's just a way to trick your mind and reduce the force required to start. 

A tiny action has almost no resistance. You get moving. And once you're moving, physics is on your side.

The smallest unit carries the biggest breakthrough.

ONE PRACTICE

A famous psychologist named Peter Gollwitzer spent years on this question: we all set goals, but then why do some people get there and others fail to achieve them? 

When it comes to your toughest, highest-friction goals, people with if-then triggers finished their hardest goals about three times as often. My roommate kept her shoes next to her bed. If the alarm went off at 5:30, then she put on her shoes and went for a run. If this, then that.  

Simple. 

So take the goal that scares you, and shrink the next move until it's almost too small to count. Not the book, three sentences. Not the workout, the shoes.

Triggers work for negative habits too. 

Pick your worst recurring habit. The snack. The doom scrolls. The procrastination loop. Instead of preventing it, choose one specific positive action you'll do the instant you catch yourself. The action has to be small. It has to be easy. And hopefully, it should be beneficial. 

You picked up that phone even when you didn’t want to. Ok fine. Now stand up. Go to the kitchen. Get a glass of water. Come back.

Try it for 21 days.

Remember what my crazy friend shared about his Everest expedition. Keep your head down. Take one step. Take a pause. And only when you’re ready for the next one, take the next one.

That’s how you climb a mountain. That’s how you build a life.
